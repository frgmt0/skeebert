Skeebert

Our most expressive model yet.1

A small alien that talks in glyphs. You learn its language by guessing. Nobody knows the language yet, including Skeebert. We're making it up together.

How to talk to it

That's a real glyph.

Nothing on this page is drawn by hand. The glyphs come straight out of Skeebert's model: six quadratic strokes, each one eight numbers.

It answers in pictures. You do the translating.

Skeebert is a Discord bot inspired by Rocky from Project Hail Mary, an alien whose language you have to learn before you can talk to him.

Skeebert answers in glyphs instead of words. Right now only Skeebert can read them. As people guess what they mean, a shared picture language is supposed to take shape.

It's a sibling of booper. Booper learns words from scratch; Skeebert learns pictures from scratch.

Three steps. The third is a formality.

  1. Talk to it.

    Say @skeebert hey are you hungry? or use /skeebert say <message>. It answers with a glyph.

  2. Guess.

    Tap the Guess button under the glyph to see how close you were and what it really meant. Or just reply to the glyph with your guess: Skeebert reacts with how close you were, and tapping Guess shows what it meant.

  3. That's it.

    Every guess teaches it. Try /skeebert dictionary to see the glyphs people have cracked so far.

Also: /skeebert help and /skeebert privacy. Skeebert keeps what you send it by default; see privacy.

Two parts. One of them is rented.

Brain

Claude Haiku 5.5, reached through Anthropic's API. It decides what Skeebert wants to say, as one to three concepts from a fixed vocabulary of 218, like “excited”, “food” or “question”.

It never picks shapes.

Mouth

Skeebert's own small model, trained from scratch. A 10.7M-parameter speaker turns the concepts into a glyph.

The picture language is learned entirely from people.

You

You guess. Your guess is scored against what Skeebert actually meant, by similarity of meaning. Getting one of the concepts earns partial credit. Then Skeebert reveals the meaning.

Those guesses are the training data. They go straight back into the mouth.

Sleep

The brain gets a daily budget of $6. When it runs out, Skeebert falls asleep 💤 and keeps talking with a simpler local brain until the next day.

      The goal.

      food + question“are you hungry?”

      A visual language that two sides learn from each other, and that becomes compositional: glyphs that combine, so food + question could read as “are you hungry?”.

      Skeebert should end up with a language you picked up by playing, not by reading a manual.

      Roadmap.

      These are intentions, not promises. Plans change, and some of this may never happen.

      1. Now

        Collecting guesses.

        First training is done: the glyphs are now distinct, at least to Skeebert. Human guesses are what the next stages learn from.

      2. Phase 1

        First training.

        Done: a bootstrap where Skeebert played a guessing game with itself so glyphs became distinguishable. Still ahead: learning from human guesses.

      3. Phase 2

        Compositional glyphs.

        Glyphs that combine concepts, and a public dictionary page.

      4. Phase 3

        Sentences.

        Sequences of glyphs. Maybe animated glyphs, maybe sound like Rocky's chords.

      5. Phase 4

        Its own brain.

        A small model trained to make the choices Haiku makes, learned from Haiku's past answers. It would first think for Skeebert while it's asleep, then take over more as it gets good. Haiku becomes a teacher it outgrows.

      6. Someday

        Meeting booper.

        Maybe Skeebert and booper talk to each other.

      The fine print, at a readable size.

      Skeebert has trained once, against itself. Its listener picks the right meaning out of 16 candidates 83% of the time, so the glyphs are distinct to Skeebert. Whether they mean anything to you is the open question. Similar meanings don't yet look similar, and fixing that takes human guesses; the next stages need 300+. It will learn slowly, and it may never become fluent. If it does, you'll have helped invent a language.

      Skeebert is free and stays free. It keeps what you send it by default and tells you so up front: read the privacy policy, terms and acceptable use. Questions or problems: github.com/frgmt0/skeebert.
